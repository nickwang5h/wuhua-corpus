When you say “me”, where do you draw the boundary? Your body is an obvious place to start. But a day also depends on air, food, other people, and a world you did not make.

Here is a Chinese expression that opens the view a little wider:

<Phrase chinese="万物" pinyin="wàn wù" gloss="all things; the countless things of the world" />

万 literally means “ten thousand”, and 物 means “thing”. Together, they can point to all the many things that exist. You do not need to count them.

## A word you might read

万物 often sounds more literary than a word you would use for the objects on your desk. You might meet it in writing about nature or the changing seasons.

<Phrase chinese="春天来了。" pinyin="chūn tiān lái le" gloss="Spring has arrived." />

This is an ordinary modern sentence. As you imagine it, notice how much more than one person is changing: plants, light, birds, the temperature of the air. 万物 offers a way to widen that picture.

## What happens to “I”?

The *Zhuangzi*, in the chapter often called “Discussion on Making All Things Equal”, offers a more surprising thought:

<ClassicQuote source="main" />

In an independent English rendering: heaven and earth come into being with me, and all things form one with me.

The surrounding passage unsettles familiar comparisons of size and length of life. Then, after speaking of “one”, it asks what happens when we put that oneness into words. Does naming it introduce another distinction?

So this line is not simply a final slogan that “everything is the same”. It invites us to notice both our connections and the boundaries our words create.

You can leave the question open. For a moment, pay attention to something around you that usually stays in the background. What changes when it becomes part of the picture?
