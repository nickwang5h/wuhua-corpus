<Phrase />

万 means “ten thousand”, but 万物 refers broadly to all things. It is a literary expression, not the usual word for the objects on your desk.

<Phrase chinese="我也是自然的一部分。" pinyin="wǒ yě shì zì rán de yī bù fen." gloss="I am part of nature too." />

我也是 means “I am also”; 一部分 means “a part”. Here 自然 has its modern meaning, “nature”. This sentence offers a familiar starting point, not a translation of the philosophical claim below.

<ClassicQuote source="main" />

Heaven and earth come into being with me, and all things form one with me.

The Zhuangzi challenges the habit of setting “me” apart from everything else. The surrounding passage questions familiar measures of size and lifespan; after speaking of “one”, it asks whether saying so creates another distinction.

This is more demanding than “everything is the same”. It invites us to reconsider the boundaries we draw, without pretending that differences disappear.

When you describe yourself as part of the world, rather than facing it from outside, what changes?
